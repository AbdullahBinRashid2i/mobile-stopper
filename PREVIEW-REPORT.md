### (c) Example root-cause chain

**Illustrative problem:** A student plans a short phone break or intends to sleep, then continues scrolling.

1. Why does use continue? One item leads into another and the student loses track of time.
2. Why is the student looking for another item? The examples describe wanting entertainment, connection, or distraction from stress and fatigue.
3. Why does distraction feel easier than stopping or starting work? The next academic step may feel unclear or effortful.
4. Why does an app timer not solve it by itself? The examples describe reminders being dismissed or lacking a replacement routine.
5. Why might the pattern repeat? Delay can add pressure and fatigue, which can make the next difficult task feel harder. This is a hypothesis to check with real participants.

**Example point of view:** Students who feel tired or pressured and use social apps to distract themselves need a low-pressure way to pause and choose their next step, because reminders alone may be easy to ignore and may not address the need for rest, connection, or a clearer task.

**How might we…**

- Help a student begin one manageable piece of a stressful assignment?
- Help someone return to their original intention after checking a notification?
- Make it easier to wind down without guilt when a student has stayed online longer than planned?

## Q3. Ideate, test and iterate

### (a) Ten possible ideas

1. A one-step task starter that converts “study” into a tiny action.
2. A flexible focus timer that can be paused or stopped.
3. A “why did I open this?” intention prompt.
4. Optional notification boundary checklist.
5. Student-controlled bedtime cue.
6. A simple wind-down routine with offline alternatives.
7. A nonjudgmental daily reflection.
8. A weekly personal pattern summary stored locally.
9. A peer study check-in that shares only when the student chooses.
10. A campus support directory with verified contact details.

**Holistic concept:** Steady combines a task starter, optional notification boundaries, a flexible focus timer, a bedtime cue, a brief private check-in, and a clear prompt to contact a real person. It supports choice across studying, interruptions, and sleep routines rather than treating screen time as the only problem.

### (b) Feature choice and trade-offs

| Feature | Desirability | Feasibility | Viability | Safety/ethics | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Task starter | High in sample answers about study avoidance | High | High | Low risk; user controlled | Include |
| Flexible timer | Medium; examples say strict timers can be ignored | High | High | Avoid locking or reporting use | Include as optional |
| Bedtime cue | High in bedtime examples | High | High | Avoid promising sleep or treatment | Include |
| Notification boundary checklist | Medium | Medium; browser cannot silence other apps | High | Explain that it does not control the phone | Include as a self-prompt |
| Public leaderboard | Not supported by examples | Medium | Medium | Could shame or expose people | Cut |
| Staff/parent monitoring | Not requested | Medium | Low | Invades privacy | Cut |
| Automatic app blocking | Not supported by evidence | Low in a web prototype | Medium | Could interfere with needed contact | Cut |
| Mental-health diagnosis score | Not supported | Low | Low | Unsafe and misleading | Cut |

## Q4. Design and implementation

### (a) Working prototype

**Steady** is a local web prototype in `index.html`. Open the file in a browser. It includes a task starter, an optional notification-boundary checklist, a flexible focus timer, bedtime prompts, a private check-in, and a reminder to contact a trusted person. The `README.md` file is the short user guide.

### (b) Feature-to-evidence link — demonstration only

| Feature | Example root cause addressed | Evidence used in this preview |
|---|---|---|
| Task starter | A task feels too large or unclear | Fictional sample answers mention delaying difficult or confusing work. |
| Optional focus timer | Student wants a bounded focus session but needs control | Fictional sample answers say timers can be dismissed; timer is not a lock. |
| Notification boundary checklist | Notifications can interrupt the intended task | General design hypothesis; must be checked with real local interviews. |
| Bedtime cue | No clear transition from phone to sleep | Fictional sample answers mention late-night use and routines. |
| Check-in and human-support prompt | Student needs to name what would help or contact a person | Safety-by-design choice; not a claim from the sample that students need counseling. |

### (c) Range of problems

The concept addresses task avoidance, interruptions, bedtime routines, and moments when the student may want help. It does not claim to address harassment, inappropriate content, serious mental-health conditions, or every form of gaming/social-media use; those need direct evidence and appropriate support.

### (d) Safety by design

The prototype has no sign-in, tracking, score, sharing, or staff/parent monitoring. It does not diagnose or provide counseling. It invites the student to contact a trusted person or campus counselor. Add the institution’s actual support contacts and verify them before a class demonstration.
